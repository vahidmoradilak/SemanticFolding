\begin{abstract}
This chapter presents a comprehensive evaluation of the Semantic Folding
(SF) paradigm for cross-lingual information retrieval on the Quran corpus.
Semantic Folding is a brain-inspired approach that maps term-context
co-occurrence patterns onto a 2D grid and constructs sparse binary
fingerprints for both phrases and documents. We evaluate SF against two
strong baselines---BM25 and a multilingual dense retriever
(paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2)---on a collection of 30 English
queries with relevance judgments across 6,236 Arabic Quranic verses.
Experimental results show that SF achieves a Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)
of 0.3454, significantly outperforming BM25 (0.1550) and the dense
retriever (0.003). We further investigate Query Expansion with Arabic
synonyms and Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) as potential improvements,
finding that RRF yields a modest 3.4\% MRR gain to 0.3571. Our analysis
reveals that SF excels on proper-name and story-based queries but
struggles with thematic concept queries where English-to-Arabic
vocabulary gaps persist. A detailed failure analysis identifies
morphological complexity and polysemy as the primary obstacles for
concept-based retrieval.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ===========================================================================

The Quran is one of the most widely studied texts in human history,
with over 1.8 billion adherents who seek guidance from its 6,236 verses.
The growing digitization of Islamic scholarship has created a pressing
need for effective information retrieval systems that can answer natural
language queries about Quranic content. However, this task presents
unique challenges: queries may be in English while the source text is
in Arabic, and the semantic richness of Quranic Arabic---with its
morphological complexity, synonymy, and contextual nuance---defies
simple keyword-based approaches.

Traditional term-matching approaches such as BM25~\cite{robertson2009bm25}
operate on lexical overlap, which is inherently limited in cross-lingual
settings. More recently, multilingual dense retrievers based on Sentence
Transformers~\cite{reimers2019sentence} have shown promise for cross-
lingual tasks, but their performance degrades when the language pair
exhibits significant structural differences, as we demonstrate in our
experiments.

Semantic Folding (SF)~\cite{webber2018semantic} offers an alternative
paradigm inspired by the brain's cortical organization. Instead of
treating documents as bags of words, SF models the distributional
semantics of terms through their co-occurrence patterns and projects
these onto a topographic 2D map---analogous to how the brain organizes
sensory input across cortical sheets. Documents and queries are then
encoded as sparse binary fingerprints whose similarity can be measured
through efficient bitwise operations.

\subsection{Contributions}

Our main contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A complete implementation of the Semantic Folding pipeline for
        the Quran corpus, including Arabic-English phrase extraction,
        term-context matrix construction, dimensionality reduction via
        UMAP, and sparse fingerprint generation.
  \item A benchmark suite of 30 English queries with graded relevance
        judgments covering six categories: surah identification, prophet
        stories, thematic concepts, and narrative passages.
  \item Comparative evaluation against BM25 and a multilingual dense
        retriever, showing that SF achieves 2.2$\times$ higher MRR than
        BM25 (0.3454 vs.~0.1550).
  \item Ablation studies examining query expansion with Arabic synonyms
        and Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) for combining SF and BM25
        rankings.
  \item Detailed failure analysis identifying thematic concept queries
        as the primary remaining challenge.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Information Retrieval for Arabic Texts}

Arabic information retrieval has received considerable attention due to
the language's rich morphology. Standard approaches include
root-based stemming~\cite{taghva2005arabic}, light stemming
\cite{larkey2002stemming}, and statistical language
models~\cite{zhai2008statistical}. BM25~\cite{robertson2009bm25} remains
a competitive baseline across Arabic IR tasks~\cite{benzahia2020bm25}.

Cross-lingual retrieval (English query, Arabic corpus) introduces
additional complexity. Translation-based approaches suffer from ambiguity
and resource requirements, while embedding-based approaches depend on
the availability of parallel corpora~\cite{aly2022cross}. The lack of
large-scale English-Arabic retrieval benchmarks further complicates
evaluation.

\subsection{Semantic Folding}

Semantic Folding was introduced by Francisco
Webber~\cite{webber2018semantic} as a brain-inspired approach to natural
language understanding. The method constructs a ``semantic space'' by
analyzing term-context co-occurrence patterns, then folds this high-
dimensional space onto a 2D grid using dimensionality reduction
techniques such as t-SNE~\cite{maaten2008tsne} or
UMAP~\cite{mcinnes2018umap}.

Each grid cell represents a ``semantic fingerprint''---a set of terms
that share similar contextual distributions. Documents are encoded by
aggregating the fingerprints of their constituent phrases, producing a
sparse binary vector that captures the document's semantic content.
Previous applications of SF include question
answering~\cite{widis2006semantic}, text classification, and sentiment
analysis, but its application to Arabic cross-lingual retrieval has not
been systematically explored.

\subsection{Dense Retrieval}

Dense retrieval models encode queries and documents into continuous
vector representations and measure relevance via cosine similarity.
Multilingual models such as
paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2~\cite{reimers2019sentence}
support 50+ languages in a shared embedding space, enabling cross-lingual
retrieval without explicit translation. However, these models are
typically trained on paraphrase pairs from Wikipedia and news corpora,
and may not capture the specialized vocabulary and stylistic register
of sacred texts.

\subsection{Stage 1: Phrase Extraction}

The first stage extracts meaningful phrases from the bilingual corpus
(Arabic verse + English translation per line). For Arabic, we use
part-of-speech (POS) pattern matching with NLTK, combined with a
function-word filter to extract noun phrases, verb phrases, and
adjective-noun bigrams. For English, we use spaCy's dependency parser
to identify noun chunks, compound chains, and left-modifier expansions.

\subsection{Stage 2: Term-Context Matrix}

Each extracted phrase serves as a ``term'' and each document (verse)
as a ``context''. We construct a term-context matrix
$M \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times D}$ where $M_{ij}$ is the frequency of
term $i$ in document $j$. TF-IDF weighting is then applied:

\begin{equation}
  w_{ij} = \text{tf}_{ij} \times \log\frac{D}{\text{df}_i}
\end{equation}

where $\text{tf}_{ij}$ is the term frequency and $\text{df}_i$ is the
document frequency of term $i$. The vocabulary contains 47,018 terms.

\subsection{Stage 3: Semantic Space Construction}

We reduce the high-dimensional term-context matrix to 2D using UMAP:

\begin{equation}
  \text{coordinates} = \text{UMAP}(M, n\_components=2,
  n\_neighbors=15, min\_dist=0.1)
\end{equation}

The resulting 2D coordinates are quantized onto a $64 \times 64$ grid
(4,096 cells), with Morton Z-order encoding to preserve spatial
locality during fingerprint generation. Collisions (multiple terms
mapping to the same grid cell) are resolved by Z-order curve
interleaving.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
  \caption{Fingerprint generation for a single phrase.}
  \label{alg:fingerprint}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \State \textbf{Input:} Grid coordinates $(x, y)$, smoothing
            $\sigma=1.5$
    \State \textbf{Output:} Binary fingerprint $F \in \{0,1\}^{4096}$
    \State \quad
    \State $G \gets \text{GaussianKernel}(3\sigma, \sigma)$
    \State $heatmap \gets \text{Zeros}(64, 64)$
    \State $heatmap[x, y] \gets 1$
    \State $heatmap \gets \text{Convolve}(heatmap, G)$
    \State $threshold \gets \text{Percentile}(heatmap[heatmap>0], 0.95)$
    \State $peaks \gets heatmap > threshold$
    \State $F \gets \text{Flatten}(\text{MortonEncode}(peaks))$
    \State \Return $F$
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Stages 4-5: Fingerprint Generation}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:fingerprint} shows the fingerprint generation process
for each phrase. Document fingerprints are formed by summing the
fingerprints of their constituent phrases with IDF weighting and L2
normalization:

\begin{equation}
  F_{\text{doc}} =
  \frac{\sum_{p \in \text{doc}} w_p \cdot F_p}
       {\|\sum_{p \in \text{doc}} w_p \cdot F_p\|_2}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Stage 7: Query Processing}

Queries undergo the same processing pipeline as corpus documents. A
simplification step extracts key content terms:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Tokenize the English query and filter by vocabulary membership
  \item Remove generic verbs and common words (e.g., ``say'',
        ``describe'', ``quran'', ``prophet'')
  \item Apply singularization (e.g., ``angels''$\rightarrow$``angel'')
  \item Append Arabic synonyms for concept terms (optional)
\end{enumerate}

A spreading operation expands the query fingerprint's active cells using
Moore neighborhood with radius 1 and decay factor 0.5, improving recall
at the cost of precision.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
  \caption{RRF fusion of SF and BM25 rankings.}
  \label{alg:rrf}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \State \textbf{Input:} SF ranked list $R_{SF}$, BM25 ranked list
            $R_{BM25}$, constant $k$
    \State \textbf{Output:} Fused ranked list $R_{RRF}$
    \State \quad
    \State $scores \gets \text{EmptyDict}()$
    \For{each $(doc_id, rank)$ in $\text{Enumerate}(R_{SF}, 1)$}
      \State $scores[doc_id] \gets scores[doc_id] + 1/(k + rank)$
    \EndFor
    \For{each $(doc_id, rank)$ in $\text{Enumerate}(R_{BM25}, 1)$}
      \State $scores[doc_id] \gets scores[doc_id] + 1/(k + rank)$
    \EndFor
    \State $R_{RRF} \gets \text{SortByDescending}(scores)$
    \State \Return $R_{RRF}$
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Baselines}

We compare SF against two baselines:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{BM25}: Okapi BM25 with standard parameters ($k_1=1.5$,
        $b=0.75$), operating on English text only. This serves as our
        lexical baseline and is computed from the same corpus.
  \item \textbf{Dense Retrieval}: The Sentence Transformer model
        paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2~\cite{reimers2019sentence},
        a 384-dimensional multilingual model. Both query and documents
        are encoded into a shared embedding space and ranked by cosine
        similarity. We tested this on three settings: (1) Arabic-only
        text, (2) English-only text, and (3) concatenated Arabic+English.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We report the following metrics, computed at $k=5$ and $k=10$:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)}: the average of reciprocal
        ranks of the first relevant document.
  \item \textbf{Average Precision (AP)}: the average of precision scores
        after each relevant document.
  \item \textbf{Precision@k (P@k)}: proportion of relevant documents
        in the top-$k$ results.
  \item \textbf{Recall@k (R@k)}: proportion of relevant documents
        retrieved in the top-$k$.
  \item \textbf{NDCG@k (NDCG@k)}: Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Implementation Details}

The pipeline is implemented in Python 3.12 with dependencies on
NumPy, SciPy, spaCy (en\_core\_web\_sm), scikit-learn, NLTK, and
PyYAML. All experiments were run on an Intel\textregistered
Core\texttrademark i7 processor with 16 GB RAM, without GPU
acceleration.

\section{Failure Analysis}

\subsection{Concept Query Failure Patterns}

Nine of fourteen concept queries achieve MRR=0 (Table~\ref{tab:failure}).
We categorize the failure modes into three patterns:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Vocabulary gap}: The concept key term exists in the
        pipeline vocabulary but its semantic activation on the grid is
        too diffuse to yield discriminative fingerprints.
  \item \textbf{Cross-lingual mismatch}: The English query concept
        (e.g., ``mercy'') maps to a different Arabic term pattern
        (رحمه) which the vocabulary treats as separate from its
        morphological variants.
  \item \textbf{Query simplification side-effect}: The
        \_extract\_key\_query\_terms function strips verbs and generic
        nouns, leaving only broad terms whose fingerprint coverage
        over-generates to non-relevant verses.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
% ===========================================================================

This chapter presented a systematic evaluation of Semantic Folding for
cross-lingual Quran retrieval. Our experiments demonstrate that SF
significantly outperforms both BM25 (2.2$\times$ MRR improvement) and
multilingual dense retrievers on the 30-query Quran benchmark. The
method's strength lies in its ability to capture distributional semantics
through spatial organization on a 2D grid, which proves particularly
effective for proper-name and story-based queries where 75\% of such
queries achieve MRR~$>$~0.5.

\begin{table}[h]
  \centering
  \caption{Final ranking of all evaluated retrieval methods.}
  \label{tab:ranking}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Rank} & \textbf{Method} & \textbf{MRR} &
    \textbf{vs.~SF}\\
    \midrule
    1 & RRF (SF + BM25) & 0.3571 & --- \\
    2 & SF (top-10) & 0.3454 & --- \\
    3 & SF (baseline) & 0.3344 & --- \\
    4 & BM25 & 0.1550 & $-1.2\times$ \\
    5 & Dense (Arabic) & 0.0030 & $-115\times$ \\
    6 & Dense (English) & $<$0.001 & $-345\times$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

However, thematic concept queries remain challenging, with 9 of 14
concept queries achieving zero MRR. This limitation stems from the
inherent difficulty of mapping broad abstract concepts into a fixed set
of spatial coordinates, compounded by Arabic's morphological complexity
and polysemy.

Future work should explore the following directions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Dense re-ranking}: Use SF as a first-pass retriever (high
        recall) followed by a multilingual Sentence Transformer for
        second-pass re-ranking of the top-$k$ candidates.
  \item \textbf{Parameter adaptation}: Adjust grid size, spreading steps,
        and top-percent thresholds specifically for concept queries,
        which may benefit from coarser spatial resolution.
  \item \textbf{Morphological analysis}: Apply Arabic morphological
        disambiguation during phrase extraction to handle clitics,
        prefixes, and suffixes that currently fragment the vocabulary.
  \item \textbf{Larger-scale evaluation}: Extend the benchmark to include
        the full MuSiQue dataset and standard IR test collections for
        Arabic.
  \item \textbf{Geometric scoring}: Apply the optional 3$\times$3
        spatial adjacency kernel before scoring to reward nearby (not
        just exact) cell overlap on the 2D grid.
\end{enumerate}
